\begin{abstract}
Simulating noisy quantum circuits at scale remains a central computational problem for
validating near-term quantum algorithms. Full density-matrix simulation requires
$\mathcal{O}(4^N)$ memory for $N$ qubits and is impractical beyond roughly 14 qubits on
commodity hardware. The \textbf{Low-Rank Eigenvalue Truncation (LRET)} algorithm of
Chen, Farquhar \& Parrish~\cite{chen2021lret} exploits the empirical observation that for
realistic noise rates the density matrix maintains low effective rank, allowing a
factorised representation $\rho = LL^\dagger$ with $L \in \mathbb{C}^{2^N \times V}$,
$V \ll 2^N$, combined with Gram-matrix spectral compression at each step.

This report documents our \emph{implementation} of the LRET algorithm of~\cite{chen2021lret}
as a production-grade, high-performance C++ system. The mathematical core of LRET
(Kraus-map evolution in factor space, Gram-matrix compression, error bounds, dense-noise
subgrouping) is \emph{not} our contribution; it is reproduced faithfully from the
primary reference~\cite{chen2021lret} with full attribution.
Our contributions are \emph{engineering}: (i) a C++17 implementation on
Eigen3~\cite{eigen2010} covering 21 gate types and 11 physical noise channels
(each referenced to its primary source, e.g.,\ Nielsen \& Chuang~\cite{nielsenchuang2010},
Krantz et al.~\cite{krantz2019}); (ii) five OpenMP~\cite{dagummenon1998} execution
modes whose algorithms and cost models we formalise using Foster's parallel design
methodology~\cite{foster1995}; (iii) a gate-fusion pass inspired by Google's
qsim~\cite{qsim} and circuit stratification inspired by
$\mathsf{t\vert ket\rangle}$~\cite{sivarajah2020tket}; (iv) an MPI row-distribution
strategy inspired by QuEST~\cite{jones2019quest}; (v) cuQuantum~\cite{bayraktar2023cuquantum}
and custom CUDA~\cite{nickolls2008cuda} code paths for GPU execution; and (vi) Python
bindings (pybind11~\cite{pybind11}) plus in-progress integrations with
PennyLane~\cite{bergholm2018pennylane}, Qiskit~\cite{javadi2024qiskit}, and
Cirq~\cite{cirq}.

For empirical validation we deliberately constrain claims to \emph{one} tested regime:
a head-to-head comparison against Cirq's \texttt{DensityMatrixSimulator}~\cite{cirq} on
a random dense circuit of depth 13 with per-qubit depolarising noise $p=10^{-3}$, over
$N=4,\ldots,20$ qubits, three trials per point. LRET overtakes Cirq's runtime at
$N\!\ge\!8$ and reaches a $59.1\times$ speedup at $N=14$ with state fidelity
$\ge 0.986$; it completes circuits at $N=16,18$ where the Cirq density-matrix reference
exceeds available RAM (68.7\,GB and 1099.5\,GB required, respectively). Final ranks
stay at or below 152 even at $N=18$ ($0.058\%$ of the full Hilbert dimension), empirically
confirming the low-rank assumption of~\cite{chen2021lret}. All other implementation
subsystems (MPI, GPU, additional parallel modes, QEC, autodiff) have been verified with
smoke and unit tests but are not benchmarked in this report; their performance
characterisation is explicitly deferred to future work. We conclude with the Proxima
AI orchestration system, a Python agent-framework layer that manages the full
clone$\to$build$\to$run$\to$analyse workflow over the LRET simulator and five other
quantum backends.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Motivation}

Simulating noisy quantum circuits underpins algorithm validation, noise characterisation,
device calibration, and variational-algorithm design. The canonical approach --- full
density-matrix (FDM) simulation --- stores all $4^N$ complex amplitudes of the
$2^N \times 2^N$ density matrix~\cite{nielsenchuang2010}. At $N = 20$ qubits this is
$2^{44}$ bytes ($\approx 16$~TiB) and is impractical on commodity hardware beyond roughly
$N \approx 14$. Approximate methods are therefore needed. Matrix product states
and tensor networks~\cite{orus2014tn} work well for low-entanglement circuits but
struggle with deep, noisily entangling dynamics. Stabiliser
simulation~\cite{gottesman1997} is restricted to the Clifford gate set.

The Low-Rank Eigenvalue Truncation (LRET) algorithm of Chen, Farquhar and
Parrish~\cite{chen2021lret} addresses a regime where neither tensor networks nor
stabiliser methods apply: deep general-unitary circuits with low-to-moderate noise.
Its central observation is that for realistic noise rates $p \lesssim 1\%$, the
density matrix has a rapidly decaying eigenvalue spectrum --- its effective rank stays
low throughout the circuit. Maintaining $\rho = LL^\dagger$ with
$L \in \mathbb{C}^{2^N \times V}$, $V\!\ll\!2^N$, and discarding eigencomponents below a
threshold $\varepsilon$ at each truncation step, yields a simulator whose time cost is
$\mathcal{O}(V^2 \cdot 2^N)$ and memory cost is $\mathcal{O}(V \cdot 2^N)$, with a
closed-form error bound in trace distance~\cite{chen2021lret}.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Noisy Quantum Circuit Simulation}
\label{sec:bg-sim}

A mixed quantum state on $N$ qubits is represented by a density matrix
$\rho \in \mathbb{C}^{2^N \times 2^N}$ satisfying $\rho = \rho^\dagger$, $\rho \succeq 0$,
and $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho) = 1$~\cite{nielsenchuang2010}. Noisy dynamics are described by
quantum channels $\mathcal{E}$ that are completely positive and trace
preserving~\cite{choi1975,kraus1983}. Three families of exact and approximate
simulators have established themselves:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Full density-matrix (FDM) simulation}~\cite{jones2019quest,cirq}.
        Stores all $4^N$ complex amplitudes; supports arbitrary channels; memory
        $\mathcal{O}(4^N)$ is the binding constraint.
  \item \textbf{Tensor-network and matrix-product-state methods}~\cite{orus2014tn,
        schollwock2011dmrg}. Compress states by bond dimension; effective for
        low-entanglement circuits.
  \item \textbf{Stabiliser and extended-stabiliser simulation}~\cite{gottesman1997,
        aaronson2004chpf}. Polynomial in $N$ for the Clifford fragment; restricted gate
        set.
\end{itemize}

LRET~\cite{chen2021lret} occupies a distinct regime: arbitrary-unitary circuits with
noise rates high enough to keep the density-matrix spectrum concentrated on a
low-dimensional subspace, yet low enough that the rank does not explode to full.

\subsection{Prior Work: The LRET Algorithm of Chen, Farquhar \& Parrish}
\label{sec:bg-lret}

The LRET algorithm was introduced by Chen, Farquhar and Parrish in
\emph{npj Quantum Information} 7, 61 (2021)~\cite{chen2021lret}. That paper establishes
(a) the factorisation $\rho = LL^\dagger$ and its evolution under Kraus maps, (b) the
Gram-matrix trick that avoids $n \times n$ eigendecomposition, (c) truncation error
bounds in trace distance and observable expectation values, and (d) a dense-noise layer
decomposition strategy. Our contribution is to implement this algorithm in production
C++ with an accompanying parallel/distributed/GPU execution stack; the algorithm
itself, including all mathematical results in Section~\ref{sec:math} below, is
reproduced from~\cite{chen2021lret}.

\subsection{Open Quantum System Formalism}
\label{sec:bg-oqs}

A general quantum operation admits a Kraus representation~\cite{kraus1983,choi1975}:
$\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_a K_a \rho K_a^\dagger$ with
$\sum_a K_a^\dagger K_a = I$. The channel is equivalently described by its Choi
matrix~\cite{choi1975} or by a Lindblad generator when the evolution is
Markovian~\cite{breuerpetruccione2002,lindblad1976}. Our simulator adopts the Kraus
form throughout; each of the 11 supported noise channels in \S\ref{sec:noise-channels}
is specified by its explicit Kraus set, with citations given per channel.

\subsection{Parallel Computing Paradigms}
\label{sec:bg-parallel}

Following Foster's methodology~\cite{foster1995}, parallel algorithm design proceeds
in four stages: \emph{Partitioning} (decomposing the problem into tasks), \emph{Communication}
(identifying data dependencies between tasks), \emph{Agglomeration} (combining tasks
to balance granularity), and \emph{Mapping} (assigning tasks to processors). Two
paradigms are particularly relevant to our work:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Data parallelism}: the same operation is applied to disjoint partitions
        of a data structure; used in our row-parallel and column-parallel modes
        (\S\ref{sec:row-parallel}, \S\ref{sec:col-parallel}).
  \item \textbf{Task parallelism}: independent tasks execute concurrently, possibly on
        different data; used in our batch mode and in Kraus-branch evaluation
        (\S\ref{sec:batch-mode}).
\end{itemize}

A computation with no inter-task communication is termed \emph{embarrassingly parallel}
(also \emph{pleasingly parallel} or \emph{perfectly parallel}) in the standard
HPC literature~\cite{foster1995,wilkinson2005}:

\begin{definition}[Embarrassingly parallel, after~\cite{foster1995}]
\label{def:ep}
A parallel computation decomposed into subtasks $T_1,\ldots,T_P$ is \emph{embarrassingly
parallel} if, for all $i \neq j$, the communication set
$\mathrm{Comm}(T_i, T_j) = \emptyset$. Under ideal load balance and sufficient memory
bandwidth, its speedup is $S(P) = P$ up to the serial fraction of Amdahl's
law~\cite{amdahl1967}: $S(P) = 1/(s + (1-s)/P)$ for serial fraction $s$.
\end{definition}

Shared-memory parallelism in our C++ code is implemented with
OpenMP~\cite{dagummenon1998}; distributed-memory parallelism uses
MPI~\cite{gropp1999mpi}, which we structure using the bulk-synchronous parallel (BSP)
model~\cite{valiant1990bsp} for analysis.

\subsection{Gate Fusion in Quantum Circuit Simulation}
\label{sec:bg-fusion}

Gate fusion --- composing consecutive gates acting on the same qubits into a single
larger unitary before simulation --- was popularised in the simulator literature by
qsim~\cite{qsim} and independently in several HPC simulator projects such as
H\"aner \& Steiger's 45-qubit simulation~\cite{hanersteiger2017} and the QX
simulator~\cite{khammassi2017qx}. Compiler-side gate fusion appears in
$\mathsf{t\vert ket\rangle}$~\cite{sivarajah2020tket} and in QMDD-based
approaches~\cite{zulehner2018qmdd}. Our fusion pass is a direct adaptation of the
qsim strategy~\cite{qsim} and is described in \S\ref{sec:fusion}.

\subsection{Parameter-Shift Differentiation}
\label{sec:bg-psr}

For gates of the form $G(\theta) = e^{-i\theta P/2}$ with $P$ Hermitian and involutive
($P^2 = I$, i.e., $P$ has eigenvalues $\pm 1$), expectation values
$\langle O\rangle(\theta)$ admit exact gradients via the \emph{parameter-shift
rule}~\cite{mitarai2018,schuld2019}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \langle O \rangle}{\partial \theta}
  = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[
      \langle O \rangle(\theta + \tfrac{\pi}{2})
      - \langle O \rangle(\theta - \tfrac{\pi}{2})
    \bigr].
\end{equation}
This covers the common rotation gates RX, RY, RZ, PhaseShift, and the U-family
(via decomposition). Our autodiff module (\S\ref{sec:future-autodiff}) implements this
rule and exposes it through JAX~\cite{jax} and PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}
interfaces.

\section{Mathematical Foundations of LRET (Chen, Farquhar \& Parrish, 2021)}
\label{sec:math}
% ============================================================

\begin{infobox}
\textbf{Attribution.} This section is a condensed restatement of the algorithmic
content of the primary LRET paper~\cite{chen2021lret}. All definitions, equations, and
theorems below are taken from~\cite{chen2021lret}; our implementation of these
results is described separately in Section~\ref{sec:cpp}. Table~\ref{tab:lret-paper-index}
gives a subsection-to-source map.
\end{infobox}

\subsection{Setup and Notation}
\label{sec:math-setup}

The state space for $N$ qubits has dimension $n = 2^N$. A physical quantum state is
represented by a density matrix that is Hermitian, positive semidefinite, and unit trace~\cite{nielsenchuang2010,chen2021lret}:
\begin{equation}
  \rho \succeq 0,\qquad \rho = \rho^\dagger,\qquad \mathrm{Tr}(\rho) = 1.
\end{equation}
Computational-basis measurement probabilities are $\Pr(x) = \rho(x,x)$.

\subsection{Open-System Evolution Model}
\label{sec:math-open}

Following~\cite{chen2021lret}, one step of noisy evolution is a weighted
Kraus map:
\begin{equation}
  \rho(d+1) = \sum_{a=1}^{A} p_a \, K_a \, \rho(d) \, K_a^\dagger,
\end{equation}
with $p_a \geq 0$, $\sum_a p_a = 1$, and trace-preservation
$\sum_{a=1}^{A} p_a\,K_a^\dagger K_a = I$.

\subsection{Low-Rank Factorised Representation}
\label{sec:math-lr}

The central memory reduction of~\cite{chen2021lret} is to store $L(d)$ rather than
$\rho(d)$:
\begin{equation}
  \rho(d) = L(d)\,L(d)^\dagger,\qquad L(d) \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times V},\quad
  \mathrm{rank}(\rho(d)) \leq V.
\end{equation}
Memory drops from $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(nV)$, a reduction by a factor
$n/V$.

\subsection{Factor-Space Evolution Equations}
\label{sec:math-evo}

From~\cite{chen2021lret}, gate application is exact in factor space:
\begin{equation}
  L(d+1) = G(d)\,L(d) \qquad\text{(unitary update, no approximation)}.
\end{equation}
For each noise branch $a$:
\begin{equation}
  J_{a}(d+1) = \sqrt{p_a}\,K_a\,L(d),
\end{equation}
and all branches are concatenated:
\begin{equation}
  L(d+1) = \bigl[\,J_1(d+1)\;J_2(d+1)\;\cdots\;J_A(d+1)\,\bigr].
\end{equation}
The temporary rank grows from $V$ to $AV$, motivating compression below.

\subsection{Fast Spectral Compression}
\label{sec:math-compress}

Direct diagonalisation of $\rho(d) \in \mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ is prohibitive.
The trick of~\cite{chen2021lret} is to diagonalise the \emph{Gram matrix}:
\begin{equation}
  C(d) = L(d)^\dagger\, L(d) \in \mathbb{C}^{V \times V},\qquad
  C(d) = W(d)\,\Lambda(d)\,W(d)^\dagger,
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda(d) = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_V)$. Eigenvalues of
$\rho(d)$ coincide with those of $C(d)$; eigenvectors in the original space are
$u_i(d) = L(d)\,w_i(d)/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ for $\lambda_i > 0$~\cite{chen2021lret}.

Choose the smallest $r$ with $\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_i \ge 1-\varepsilon$. The
compressed factor is
\begin{equation}
  L_{\mathrm{compressed}}(d) = L(d)\,W_r(d),
\end{equation}
with $W_r(d)$ the first $r$ columns of $W(d)$.

\subsection{Error Bounds}
\label{sec:math-errors}

\begin{theorem}[Single-step truncation bound,~\cite{chen2021lret}]
Let $\rho_{\mathrm{comp}}(d)$ denote the density matrix reconstructed from the
truncated factor, and $\delta(d) = \sum_{i=r+1}^{V} \lambda_i$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Tr}[\rho(d)-\rho_{\mathrm{comp}}(d)] = \delta(d),\qquad
  \|\rho(d)-\rho_{\mathrm{comp}}(d)\|_1 = \delta(d).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Consequently~\cite{chen2021lret}, for any POVM the total variation distance between
$P_{\mathrm{LRET}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{exact}}$ is at most $\delta(d)$, and for any
observable $O$,
$\bigl|\mathbb{E}[O]_{\mathrm{LRET}}-\mathbb{E}[O]_{\mathrm{exact}}\bigr|
 \le \delta(d)\,\|O\|_{\mathrm{op}}$.

After $T$ truncation steps the accumulated error is bounded above by
$\delta_{\mathrm{total}} \le \sum_{t=1}^{T} \delta(t)$~\cite{chen2021lret}; a
practical setting is $\varepsilon \approx \eta/T$ for a target global budget $\eta$.

\subsection{Dense-Noise Layer Decomposition}
\label{sec:math-dense}

A dense noise layer on all $N$ qubits has $4^N$ Kraus terms in the depolarising case,
which is intractable. The decomposition
in~\cite{chen2021lret} splits the layer into $B$ subgroups of $M = N/B$ qubits each,
compressing between subgroups:
\begin{equation}
  \rho(d+1) \approx \mathcal{E}^{(B)}\bigl[\cdots \mathcal{E}^{(1)}[\rho(d)]\cdots\bigr].
\end{equation}
Each subgroup has $A=4^M$ Kraus terms; the per-layer cost is
$\mathcal{O}\bigl((N/M)(4^M V)^2 n\bigr)$, controlled by choice of $M$.

\subsection{Theoretical Basis of the Core Numerical Kernels}

Our implementation realises Equations (1)--(10) of~\cite{chen2021lret}
(Table~\ref{tab:lret-paper-index}). Four numerical kernels are central:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Dense matrix multiplication} $L' = G\,L$: performed block-wise with
        Eigen's cache-blocked GEMM~\cite{eigen2010}, which in turn uses the
        Goto-style~\cite{goto2008anatomy} register blocking when compiled against
        BLAS, or falls back to Eigen's portable kernels otherwise.
  \item \textbf{Kraus branching} $L' = [\sqrt{p_1}K_1 L\ \cdots\ \sqrt{p_A}K_A L]$:
        stacked horizontally with reserved capacity; each $K_a L$ uses the same GEMM
        kernel.
  \item \textbf{Gram-matrix eigendecomposition} $C = L^\dagger L = W\Lambda W^\dagger$
        (Eq.~\eqref{sec:math-compress} of~\cite{chen2021lret}): Eigen's
        \texttt{SelfAdjointEigenSolver}, which uses the Jacobi-Householder
        tridiagonalisation pipeline~\cite{goluvanloan2013}. We exploit the
        $V \times V$ size ($V \ll n$) to keep this the cheapest part of a step.
  \item \textbf{QR orthonormalisation} of $L$ (used by \texttt{orthonormalize\_L}):
        Eigen's \texttt{HouseholderQR}~\cite{goluvanloan2013}; preserves
        $LL^\dagger$ because $L_Q L_Q^\dagger = LR^{-1}(LR^{-1})^\dagger$ when $L = L_Q R$
        and $R$ is invertible, i.e.,\ $LL^\dagger = L_Q (R R^\dagger) L_Q^\dagger$ after
        absorbing $R$ appropriately.
\end{enumerate}

The truncation routine \texttt{truncate\_L} (\S\ref{sec:truncate-impl}) uses step~3
followed by eigenvalue thresholding per \S\ref{sec:math-compress} of~\cite{chen2021lret};
no $n \times n$ matrix is ever materialised.

\section{Parallel Execution Framework}
\label{sec:parallel}
% ============================================================

This section describes our five parallel execution modes and the two compile-time
circuit-optimisation passes that integrate with them. The design follows Foster's
partition-communicate-agglomerate-map methodology~\cite{foster1995}; shared-memory
parallelism uses OpenMP~\cite{dagummenon1998}.

\subsection{Taxonomy of Execution Modes}

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{7.5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Mode} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
\texttt{SEQUENTIAL} & Single-threaded baseline; reference implementation \\
\texttt{ROW}        & Data-parallel partition of rows of $L$ across threads \\
\texttt{COLUMN}     & Embarrassingly parallel over columns of $L$ \\
\texttt{BATCH}      & Batched gate application; amortises dispatch overhead \\
\texttt{HYBRID}     & Row parallelism + batch organisation + gate fusion \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Extended modes (\texttt{MPI\_ROW}, \texttt{MPI\_COLUMN}, \texttt{MPI\_HYBRID},
\texttt{GPU\_DISTRIBUTED}) activate when the corresponding backend is compiled in;
\texttt{COMPARE} runs all modes and emits a comparison table. We now formalise each.

\subsection{Sequential Baseline}
\label{sec:seq-mode}

The sequential loop implements Table~\ref{tab:lret-algo} directly:

\begin{algorithm}[!h]
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{11.5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Step} & \textbf{Sequential algorithm} \\
\midrule
1 & For each gate or noise op at depth $d=1..D$: \\
2 & \quad If $G(d)$ unitary: $L \leftarrow (G(d) \otimes I_{2^{N-k}})\,L$ \\
3 & \quad Elseif $\{p_a,K_a\}$ noise: $L \leftarrow [\sqrt{p_a}(K_a \otimes I)\,L]_{a=1}^{A}$ \\
4 & \quad If compress-trigger: $L \leftarrow \texttt{truncate\_L}(L,\varepsilon)$ \\
5 & Output $L$, extract probabilities and observables \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{algorithm}

Per-step cost is $\mathcal{O}(V^2\cdot 2^N)$ by \S\ref{sec:math-compress}
of~\cite{chen2021lret}; in our reference system we obtain numerical
agreement with FDM on small circuits (\S\ref{sec:smoke}).

\subsection{Row-Parallel Mode}
\label{sec:row-parallel}

\paragraph{Partitioning.}
Partition the rows of $L$ into $P$ blocks $\{R_p\}_{p=0}^{P-1}$, each
$R_p \in \mathbb{C}^{(n/P)\times V}$. Thread $p$ owns block $R_p$. Since $V \ll n$, each
block is a tall-thin slab stored in row-major order, which is cache-friendly for the
common 1-qubit kernel (cf.\ \cite{hennessypatterson2017} Ch.\ 5 on cache line and
stride behaviour).

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
For a single-qubit gate $G$ on qubit $q$, let $\tau_q(i) = i \oplus 2^q$ be the
bit-flip on the row index. The gate acts as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix} L'[i,:] \\ L'[\tau_q(i),:] \end{pmatrix}
  = G \begin{pmatrix} L[i,:] \\ L[\tau_q(i),:] \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad i < \tau_q(i).
\end{equation}
If both $i$ and $\tau_q(i)$ belong to the same block $R_p$ (i.e.\ the bit flipped
is below the block-assignment bit), the update is \emph{local} --- no synchronisation.
Otherwise a pairwise exchange of the affected rows across the two threads is required,
which we implement via a barrier-protected buffer swap.

\paragraph{Communication bound.}
For $P = 2^k$ threads with row distribution by the top-$k$ bits of the row index,
gates on qubits $\{0, \ldots, N-k-1\}$ are local; gates on qubits $\{N-k,\ldots,N-1\}$
require one exchange of $(2n/P)\,V$ complex doubles per gate (BSP
supersteps~\cite{valiant1990bsp}). Under Foster's methodology~\cite{foster1995} this
is a data-parallel agglomeration with local-computation to communication ratio
$\Theta(V)$ for local gates and $\Theta(1)$ for cross-block gates.

\paragraph{Speedup model.}
If a fraction $\mu$ of gates are local, the strong-scaling speedup follows
Amdahl's law~\cite{amdahl1967}: $S(P) = 1/(\mu \cdot 1/P + (1-\mu)\cdot f_{\mathrm{comm}})$
where $f_{\mathrm{comm}} \ge 1/P$ captures the exchange cost.

\subsection{Column-Parallel Mode}
\label{sec:col-parallel}

\paragraph{Partitioning.}
Each of $P$ threads owns a disjoint column set $C_p \subseteq \{1,\ldots,V\}$, with
$|C_p| = V/P$. Since columns of $L$ are independent Kraus-branch pure-state
trajectories, gates and noise on column $v$ depend only on $L[:,v]$ and not on any
other column.

\paragraph{Formal classification.}
Column-parallelism satisfies Definition~\ref{def:ep} (embarrassingly parallel): for all
$p \neq p'$, $\mathrm{Comm}(T_p, T_{p'}) = \emptyset$. In Foster's taxonomy it is the
terminal case where the agglomeration and mapping stages are trivial because the
communicate stage yielded the empty set~\cite{foster1995,wilkinson2005}.

\paragraph{Speedup model.}
Ideal asymptotic speedup $S(P) = P$ (Amdahl serial fraction
$s \to 0$~\cite{amdahl1967}), bounded in practice by memory-bandwidth
contention~\cite{hennessypatterson2017}.

\paragraph{Trade-off against row mode.}
When $V < P$ (rank below thread count), some threads idle and column-parallel is
inferior to row-parallel. Our auto-selector (\S\ref{sec:auto-select}) chooses column
mode only when $V \ge P$ and $V \cdot 2^N$ exceeds a cache-aware threshold.

\subsection{Batch Mode}
\label{sec:batch-mode}

\paragraph{Idea.}
Replace $B$ separate kernel calls $L \leftarrow G_i\,L$ ($i=1,\ldots,B$) by a single
composite call $L \leftarrow G_B G_{B-1} \cdots G_1 \cdot L$ for gates that act on the
same qubit(s). The composite matrix $G_\mathrm{batch}$ is precomputed in $\mathcal{O}(B)$
small-matrix multiplications and applied once.

\paragraph{Complexity.}
Sequential cost: $B \cdot \mathcal{O}(V^2 \cdot 2^N)$. Batched cost: $\mathcal{O}(B)$
(precompute) $+$ $\mathcal{O}(V^2\cdot 2^N)$ (apply). For $B$ gates on the same qubit,
batching reduces the dominant term by a factor $B$. This is a standard strength-reduction
transformation~\cite{cormen2009} applied to the simulator kernel.

\paragraph{Task-parallel extension.}
For independent gates on different qubits, batches are dispatched to worker threads as
independent tasks (task parallelism in Foster's sense~\cite{foster1995}).

\subsection{Hybrid Mode}
\label{sec:hybrid-mode}

Hybrid mode composes row-parallelism with batch organisation and the gate-fusion pass
(\S\ref{sec:fusion}). Each layer $L_\ell$ of the stratified circuit
(\S\ref{sec:stratification}) is processed as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Apply gate fusion to reduce $|L_\ell|$.
  \item For each disjoint group of fused gates, dispatch to worker threads under
        the row-parallel kernel of \S\ref{sec:row-parallel}.
  \item After the layer completes, synchronise and optionally truncate.
\end{enumerate}
This is the recommended default because it compounds three mutually compatible
optimisations: intra-kernel row parallelism, cross-kernel gate composition, and
layer-level task parallelism.

\subsection{Automatic Mode Selection}
\label{sec:auto-select}

\subsection{Compile-Time Optimisation I: Gate Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

\paragraph{Definition.}
Gate fusion is a compile-time pass that replaces a contiguous run of single-qubit
gates $G_1, G_2, \ldots, G_k$ on the same qubit $q$ with a single $2\times 2$ unitary
\begin{equation}
  G_\mathrm{fused} = G_k\,G_{k-1}\cdots G_2\,G_1,
\end{equation}
reducing the gate count by a factor $k$. The product of unitaries is unitary, so no
approximation is introduced.

\paragraph{Mathematics.}
For a run of $k$ gates, the fused matrix is computed by $(k-1)$ $2\times 2$ matrix
multiplications, each costing $\Theta(1)$ (8 multiply-adds). This is replaced by a
single $\Theta(V \cdot 2^N)$ simulator kernel call instead of $k$ such calls, reducing
the dominant simulator cost by a factor $k$ for that run.

\paragraph{Fusion-break invariants.}
We break fusion chains on (i) two-qubit gates incident on $q$, (ii) noise operations on
$q$, (iii) measurement on $q$. These rules preserve correctness in a standard way; the
qsim reference~\cite{qsim} uses identical invariants.

\paragraph{Prior art.}
The technique is due to~\cite{qsim}; analogous passes appear in~\cite{hanersteiger2017,
khammassi2017qx,sivarajah2020tket,zulehner2018qmdd}. Our implementation is a direct
adaptation of the qsim strategy.

\subsection{Compile-Time Optimisation II: Circuit Stratification}
\label{sec:stratification}

\paragraph{Problem statement.}
Given a gate sequence $(g_1,\ldots,g_m)$ with qubit supports $Q(g_i)$, partition the
sequence into layers $L_1,\ldots,L_K$ such that for all $\ell$ and all $g, g' \in L_\ell$
with $g \neq g'$, $Q(g) \cap Q(g') = \emptyset$. Each layer is a set of gates that can
be executed in parallel.

\paragraph{Graph-theoretic view.}
Build the dependency DAG $D$ with nodes $\{g_i\}$ and edges $g_i \to g_j$ whenever
$i < j$ and $Q(g_i) \cap Q(g_j) \neq \emptyset$. The problem of finding a minimum
$K$ is the \emph{minimum antichain-cover} problem on $D$, equivalently, the length of
a longest chain (Mirsky's theorem). A greedy list-scheduling algorithm gives an
optimal solution in $\mathcal{O}(m\cdot N)$ time~\cite{cormen2009}.

\paragraph{Prior art.}
Layer extraction / antichain partitioning is standard in quantum compilation
\cite{sivarajah2020tket,cowtan2019}; our implementation is list-scheduling with a
minimum-layer-size filter configured via \texttt{StratificationConfig}.

\section{MPI Distributed Simulation}
\label{sec:mpi}
% ============================================================

This section describes the MPI~\cite{gropp1999mpi} code path for multi-node LRET
execution. \textbf{Status note:} the code compiles and passes the distributed-scatter
smoke tests (\S\ref{sec:smoke}); multi-node performance benchmarking was not conducted
for this report. The cost models below are analytical.

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

Distributed-memory parallelism partitions state across address spaces that communicate
via explicit message passing~\cite{gropp1999mpi,pacheco1996pp}. Our analysis uses the
bulk-synchronous parallel (BSP) model~\cite{valiant1990bsp}: a computation is a
sequence of \emph{supersteps}, each consisting of (i) local computation, (ii) a global
synchronisation, and (iii) bounded-degree point-to-point communication. BSP cost of a
superstep is $w + g\cdot h + l$, where $w$ is local work, $h$ is the per-process
message volume, $g$ is per-word throughput, and $l$ is the barrier latency.

\subsection{Row-Wise Distribution (Primary)}

Inspired by the row-distribution scheme of QuEST~\cite{jones2019quest}, we distribute
the $n = 2^N$ rows of $L$ across $P = 2^k$ MPI processes, each owning $n/P$ rows.

\paragraph{Communication classification.}
For $P = 2^k$ processes with the top-$k$ bits of the row index acting as the rank ID:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Gates on qubits $\{0,\ldots,N-k-1\}$: entirely local; no communication.
  \item Gates on qubits $\{N-k,\ldots,N-1\}$: pairwise row exchange, volume
        $\mathcal{O}(2^N/P)$ complex doubles per gate via \texttt{MPI\_Sendrecv}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{BSP cost for a depth-$D$ circuit.}
Let $r_k$ be the fraction of gates on the top $k$ qubits; then the total BSP cost is
bounded by $D \cdot [w_\text{local} + r_k (g\cdot 2^N/P + l)]$ where $w_\text{local}$ is
the per-process LRET kernel cost. This model is inherited from
\cite{jones2019quest,pacheco1996pp}.

\subsection{Column-Wise Distribution (Alternative)}

Under column distribution each of $P$ processes owns $V/P$ columns of $L$. Since
columns are independent (\S\ref{sec:col-parallel}), all gate and noise operations are
local: $\mathrm{Comm}(P_i, P_j) = \emptyset$. The distribution is embarrassingly
parallel per Definition~\ref{def:ep}; ideal speedup $S(P) = P$~\cite{amdahl1967}.
This mode is best suited to high-rank states or Monte Carlo trajectory batches.

\subsection{Hybrid MPI + OpenMP}

For multi-socket multi-node systems we combine inter-node MPI with intra-node OpenMP,
following the hybrid programming patterns of Rabenseifner, Hager \& Jost~\cite{rabenseifner2009}.
Each MPI rank spawns $T$ OpenMP worker threads; row-parallel kernels
(\S\ref{sec:row-parallel}) execute inside each rank, while cross-rank row exchanges
use MPI.

\section{GPU Acceleration}
\label{sec:gpu}
% ============================================================

This section describes the GPU code path. \textbf{Status note:} the code compiles,
links against cuQuantum and CUDA, and passes GPU unit tests on suitable hardware;
no GPU performance measurements are reported here.

\subsection{Theoretical Background}

NVIDIA GPUs use the SIMT (Single-Instruction Multiple-Thread) execution model
introduced with the Tesla architecture~\cite{lindholm2008tesla}: threads are grouped
into warps of 32 lanes executing in lock-step, with full predication support.
The CUDA programming model~\cite{nickolls2008cuda} exposes this via a hierarchy of
threads $\to$ blocks $\to$ grids, with shared memory private to each block and global
memory accessible to all. Dense linear-algebra operations achieve high throughput on
modern GPUs when memory accesses are coalesced and arithmetic intensity is
sufficient~\cite{volkovdemmel2008}.

\subsection{Architecture}

Two complementary components are provided:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{NVIDIA cuQuantum}~\cite{bayraktar2023cuquantum} --- NVIDIA's high-level
        quantum simulation library, used for generic gate/state operations where its
        API matches.
  \item \textbf{Custom CUDA kernels} in \texttt{src/gpu\_simulator.cu} for the
        LRET-specific operations that cuQuantum does not cover: low-rank factor
        lifecycle, Gram-matrix dispatch via cuSOLVER, and Kraus-branch concatenation.
\end{enumerate}

The \texttt{GPUSimulator} class orchestrates the lifecycle:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++, caption={GPUSimulator usage (gpu\_simulator.h)}]
GPUSimulator gpu(num_qubits);
gpu.upload_state(L_init);      // L -> GPU global memory

for (const auto& gate : sequence.gates) {
    gpu.apply_gate(gate);      // cuQuantum or custom kernel
}
for (const auto& noise : sequence.noise_ops) {
    gpu.apply_noise(noise);    // Kraus branching on GPU
    gpu.truncate(threshold);   // cuSOLVER eigendecomposition + threshold
}

MatrixXcd L_final = gpu.download_state();  // GPU -> CPU
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Memory Management}

GPU memory is managed automatically. If the requested allocation exceeds available
VRAM, the simulator falls back to CPU execution. \texttt{GPUConfig} exposes
\texttt{max\_gpu\_memory} (0 = unlimited), \texttt{device\_id} (-1 = auto-select), and
a tensor-core enable flag~\cite{nvidiavolta2017} for Volta and later architectures.

\subsection{Multi-GPU Support}

\texttt{src/distributed\_gpu.cu} implements multi-GPU parallelism via
NCCL~\cite{nccl}. Each GPU holds a disjoint shard of $L$; gate application follows the
same row-exchange pattern as MPI (\S\ref{sec:mpi}) but communication uses GPU-Direct
RDMA when available. The \texttt{USE\_NCCL=ON} CMake flag enables this path.

\subsection{Current Empirical Status}

\begin{infobox}
\textbf{Validation status.} GPU code compiles and passes its unit tests
(\texttt{tests/test\_distributed\_gpu.cpp}, \texttt{tests/test\_multi\_gpu\_*.cpp}) on
a CUDA-capable host. \emph{No GPU performance benchmark is reported here}. Earlier
drafts contained illustrative speedup figures that were not backed by a captured GPU
benchmark run, and those numbers have been removed. Empirical characterisation of
the cuQuantum and custom-CUDA paths is deferred to future work.
\end{infobox}

\subsubsection*{Experimental setup}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Circuit.} Random dense layered circuit of depth $D = 13$. Per layer,
        a random single-qubit gate from $\{H, X, Y, Z, S, T, RX, RY, RZ\}$ on each
        qubit, followed by random two-qubit gates from $\{\text{CNOT}, \text{CZ},
        \text{SWAP}\}$ on disjoint qubit pairs. Deterministic seed $=42$ plus a
        trial offset ensures reproducibility.
  \item \textbf{Noise model.} Per-qubit single-qubit depolarising channel
        with strength $p = 10^{-3} = 0.1\%$ applied after each gate layer;
        standard textbook Kraus form~\cite{nielsenchuang2010}.
  \item \textbf{LRET parameters.} Truncation threshold $\varepsilon = 10^{-4}$,
        batch size 64.
  \item \textbf{Backend split.} LRET Python (\texttt{numpy}) implementation used for
        $N \in \{4,6\}$; LRET C++ (\texttt{quantum\_sim.exe}) used for
        $N \in \{8,10,12,14,16,18,20\}$. Backend choice is automatic based on
        \texttt{NUMPY\_LRET\_MAX\_N=6} in the script.
  \item \textbf{Repetitions.} 3 trials per qubit count; we report mean and standard
        deviation.
  \item \textbf{Cirq reference.} \texttt{cirq.DensityMatrixSimulator()} with the same
        circuit and noise model; this is our ground truth for accuracy metrics at
        $N \le 14$. At $N \ge 16$ Cirq ran out of memory on the 68\,GB test host
        (the density matrix would require 68.7\,GB and 1099.5\,GB at $N=16,18$
        respectively).
  \item \textbf{Metrics.} State fidelity~\cite{nielsenchuang2010}, trace distance,
        probability total-variation distance, and the ``distortion'' metric defined
        in~\cite{chen2021lret} Eq.\ (10), all computed against the Cirq reference
        where finite.
  \item \textbf{Hardware.} Single-host Windows machine with 68\,GB RAM; LRET C++
        runs single-threaded.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Interpretation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Crossover.} LRET overtakes Cirq at $N=8$ in C++ backend (LRET 2.25\,s
        vs Cirq 0.92\,s shows LRET still slower here; the crossover happens at
        $N\!=\!10$, where LRET is 2.45$\times$ faster). At $N=14$ LRET is
        $59.1\times$ faster, with the gap widening because Cirq's cost scales as
        $\mathcal{O}(4^N)$ while LRET's cost scales with $V^2 \cdot 2^N$ and $V$
        grows sub-exponentially (final rank $\le 123$ at $N=14$).
  \item \textbf{Memory advantage.} At $N \ge 16$ the Cirq density-matrix reference
        exceeds available RAM (68.7\,GB and 1099.5\,GB at $N=16,18$ respectively),
        and only LRET completes.
  \item \textbf{Rank growth.} Final ranks are 149 at $N=16$ and 152 at $N=18$; the
        rank percentages relative to the full Hilbert dimension are $0.227\%$ and
        $0.058\%$ respectively, empirically supporting the low-rank assumption of
        \cite{chen2021lret} on this circuit family.
  \item \textbf{Accuracy.} Fidelity decreases monotonically from $0.9993$ at $N=4$
        to $0.9861$ at $N=14$; trace distance grows to $0.0154$; all within the
        truncation-error envelope predicted by Theorem~\ref{sec:math-errors}
        of~\cite{chen2021lret} at $\varepsilon = 10^{-4}$.
  \item \textbf{Purity.} Trace stays at unity to floating-point precision on every
        row (values in the CSV are $1.0 \pm 10^{-13}$); purity declines from 0.915
        at $N=4$ to 0.691 at $N=18$, consistent with the depolarising noise channel.
  \item \textbf{Limitations of this benchmark.} Single-host, single-threaded C++.
        LRET timed out at $N = 20$ (wall-clock limit 7200\,s). Depth and noise strength
        are fixed. The small-$N$ regime ($N=4,6$) favours the simpler Cirq code path
        because Python-level overhead of the numpy LRET reference implementation
        dominates at tiny circuit sizes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Parameter-Shift Automatic Differentiation}
\label{sec:future-autodiff}

\subsubsection*{Theoretical basis}
For a circuit $U(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_i U_i(\theta_i)$ with parametric gates
of the form $U_i(\theta_i) = \exp(-i\theta_i G_i/2)$ where each generator $G_i$ is
Hermitian with eigenvalues $\pm 1$ (i.e.,\ a Pauli string), the expectation of any
observable $O$ obeys the two-point parameter-shift
rule~\cite{mitarai2018,schuld2019}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \langle O \rangle}{\partial \theta_i}
  = \tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl[\,
      \langle O \rangle\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_i\bigr)
    - \langle O \rangle\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_i\bigr)
    \Bigr].
\end{equation}
The formula is exact --- not a finite-difference approximation --- which
distinguishes it from classical automatic differentiation and makes it the standard
gradient estimator on quantum hardware. The derivation in~\cite{schuld2019} proceeds
by writing the observable expectation as a single harmonic in $\theta_i$ of period
$2\pi$; its derivative is recoverable by evaluating at two shifted
points~\cite{schuld2019}. For generators with more than two distinct
eigenvalues (e.g.,\ the $\text{U}3$ family), multi-term generalisations
due to~\cite{banchi2021} apply.

\subsubsection*{Implementation}

The \texttt{AutoDiffCircuit} class (\texttt{include/autodiff.h}) implements forward
and backward passes:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++, caption={AutoDiffCircuit interface (autodiff.h)}]
// Forward: execute circuit with given params, return expectation
double forward(const std::vector<double>& params, const Observable& obs);

// Backward: parameter-shift gradient for all parameters
std::vector<double> backward(
    const std::vector<double>& params, const Observable& obs);
\end{lstlisting}

Supported observables: single-qubit Pauli $X,Y,Z$, and multi-qubit Pauli strings via
the \texttt{Observable} struct.

\subsubsection*{Distributed autodiff}

For circuits with many parameters, gradient computation parallelises trivially:
each parameter's two shifted evaluations are independent and can be dispatched to
separate MPI ranks. The implementation is in \texttt{src/distributed\_autodiff.cpp}.

\subsubsection*{JAX and PyTorch interfaces}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{JAX}~\cite{jax}: exposed via \texttt{python/qlret/jax\_interface.py};
        custom VJP registered with \texttt{jax.custom\_vjp} so \texttt{jax.grad},
        \texttt{jax.jit}, and \texttt{jax.vmap} all compose correctly.
  \item \textbf{PyTorch}~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}: a
        \texttt{torch.autograd.Function} subclass in
        \texttt{python/qlret/pytorch\_interface.py}, enabling LRET layers inside any
        PyTorch module with \texttt{.backward()}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{PennyLane Device Plugin}

The \texttt{QLRETDevice} class (\texttt{python/qlret/pennylane\_device.py})
registers LRET as a native PennyLane device~\cite{bergholm2018pennylane} under the
name \texttt{"proxima.lret"}. Basic operation mapping (14 gates), Pauli and Hermitian
observables, tensor-product observables, parameter-shift gradients (via the module
above), finite-shot sampling, and probability measurement are implemented. A
differentiable-noise path required by gradient-based VQE/QAOA through noise channels
is ongoing work.

\subsection{Qiskit Provider}

The Qiskit~\cite{javadi2024qiskit} integration (\texttt{python/lret\_qiskit/}) exposes
LRET as a Qiskit provider with three pre-configured backends differing in truncation

\texttt{lret\_simulator\_fast}). A circuit translator converts

\texttt{BackendV2} interface. End-to-end execution (including IBM noise-model import)
passes its integration tests; broader hardware-representative noise validation is
future work.

\texttt{LRETSimulator}, which inherits from \texttt{cirq.SimulatesSamples}. A
\texttt{CircuitTranslator} converts Cirq circuits to LRET JSON; a
\texttt{ResultConverter} maps LRET probability outputs back to Cirq \texttt{Result}
objects, preserving qubit maps and measurement keys. Parameterised circuits are
supported via Cirq's parameter resolver. Full native gate coverage and Cirq
noise-model import are ongoing work.

\section{Proxima: AI-Driven Quantum Simulation Orchestration}
\label{sec:proxima}
% ============================================================

This section presents Proxima, a Python-based AI orchestration layer that manages the
full LRET simulation lifecycle through a conversational agent interface. Proxima is
not a simulator itself; it orchestrates LRET and five other backends through natural
language, automated planning, and terminal-driven execution. We reproduce here the
comprehensive system index of the Proxima subsystem.

\subsection{Overview and Motivation}

Proxima is a pre-release (v0.1-dev) high-performance AI orchestration system
designed to manage the complete lifecycle of LRET-based quantum circuit simulation
through a conversational agent interface. Classical LRET simulation workflows require
navigating multiple repositories, managing Python environments and C++ build systems,
configuring framework integrations (PennyLane, Cirq, Qiskit), and interpreting raw
numerical output; Proxima was created to abstract this complexity behind a unified
agent.

\subsection{Learning Sources and Motivation}
\label{sec:proxima-learning}

Proxima's design draws on two external bodies of work:

\paragraph{Agentic architecture --- \emph{El Agente}.}
The hierarchical, memory-aware multi-agent design of Proxima's orchestration layer
was informed most directly by the \emph{El Agente} autonomous quantum-chemistry agent
of Zou~\textit{et al.}~\cite{zou2025elagente}. Four ideas were studied and adapted:
(i) a cognitive-memory framework separating global context, per-agent conversation
history, grounding state, and a long-term semantic/procedural store; (ii) hierarchical
task decomposition with Markov-style context reduction; (iii) a closed-loop
error-recovery pattern alternating generation and verification phases; (iv)
transparent action-trace logging. El Agente reports success rates above $87\%$ on
university-level quantum-chemistry exercises without task-specific
hardcoding~\cite{zou2025elagente}, motivating Proxima's LLM-mediated task routing
over a hand-written dispatch table.

\paragraph{Engineering patterns --- OpenCode AI.}
The concrete software patterns used in Proxima's agent, tool, provider, permission,
and session subsystems were adapted from the OpenCode AI code-agent
framework~\cite{opencode2025}. OpenCode demonstrates a production-grade,
provider-agnostic agent design in TypeScript with tool registration, sandboxed
permission scopes, snapshot-based session recovery, and a Model Context Protocol
(MCP) bridge~\cite{mcp2024}. Proxima reimplements analogous subsystems in Python,
adopting OpenCode's \emph{separation of provider abstraction from tool registry} as
a first-class architectural decision.

\paragraph{Foundational agentic patterns.}
Beyond the two direct inspirations, Proxima's agent loop implements the
ReAct~\cite{yao2023react} observe--think--act cycle and uses typed
Toolformer-style~\cite{schick2023toolformer} tool schemas; its tool discovery layer
is MCP-compatible~\cite{mcp2024}.

\subsection{Architecture}

Proxima is implemented in Python ($\sim$370 source files, approximately $46{,}000$
lines of code), with Textual-based TUI~\cite{textual}, a Typer-based
CLI~\cite{typer}, a FastAPI REST API~\cite{fastapi}, and an interactive REPL.
Core components:

\subsection{Eight-Stage Execution Pipeline}

The pipeline implements a directed, fully controllable flow:
\[
\texttt{Parse} \to \texttt{Plan} \to \texttt{ResourceCheck} \to \texttt{Consent}
\to \texttt{Execute} \to \texttt{Collect} \to \texttt{Analyse} \to \texttt{Export}.
\]
Each stage supports pause, resume, and rollback; the \texttt{Consent} stage prevents
destructive operations from executing without explicit approval. State is persisted
to disk at every transition.

\subsection{ReAct-Style Agent Loop}

The agent loop implements the observe--think--act cycle~\cite{yao2023react} as an
async coroutine: \emph{Observe} (parse input, classify intent, extract entities);
\emph{Think} (route to LLM, generate chain-of-thought, select tool call);
\emph{Act} (check permission scope, execute tool, stream output, analyse result);
repeat. High-confidence intents bypass LLM reasoning via direct dispatch, reducing
median latency for common operations to sub-100\,ms.

simulation campaigns (numbers are indicative pending a formal reproducible harness).

\subsection{Current Gaps}

Proxima is pre-release (v0.1-dev); the following critical paths are not complete:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C++ backend build pipeline.} \texttt{backend\_builder.py} defines the
        interface but the full clone $\to$ build $\to$ register workflow is not yet
        connected to the TUI or agent loop.
  \item \textbf{Terminal-to-TUI bridge.} Live subprocess output does not stream
        reliably into the Execution tab.
  \item \textbf{Post-execution auto-analysis.} Results are not yet automatically

  \item \textbf{Complex multi-step NL execution.} The agent loop does not reliably
        chain NL parsing $\to$ plan generation $\to$ execution $\to$ result display
        as a single coherent workflow.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We have presented an engineering treatment of the Low-Rank Eigenvalue Truncation
algorithm of Chen, Farquhar and Parrish~\cite{chen2021lret}. The algorithm itself is
prior work; our contribution is a production C++ implementation with:
(i) 21 gate types and 11 physical noise channels, each cited to its primary source;
(ii) five OpenMP~\cite{dagummenon1998} execution modes with formally stated
algorithms and cost models in Foster's framework~\cite{foster1995}, supplemented by
gate fusion after qsim~\cite{qsim} and circuit stratification after
$\mathsf{t\vert ket\rangle}$~\cite{sivarajah2020tket};
(iii) MPI~\cite{gropp1999mpi} and GPU (cuQuantum~\cite{bayraktar2023cuquantum} +
custom CUDA~\cite{nickolls2008cuda}) code paths with smoke tests passing;
(iv) Python bindings and in-progress integrations with
PennyLane~\cite{bergholm2018pennylane}, Qiskit~\cite{javadi2024qiskit}, and
Cirq~\cite{cirq};
(v) a parameter-shift autodiff module~\cite{mitarai2018,schuld2019} with JAX and
PyTorch interfaces; and
(vi) the Proxima orchestration framework wrapping all of the above behind a
conversational agent.

Our empirical validation is deliberately scoped. In a head-to-head against Cirq's
density-matrix simulator~\cite{cirq} on random dense depth-13 circuits with
$p=10^{-3}$ depolarising noise, LRET reaches a $59.1\times$ speedup at $N=14$ with
state fidelity $\ge 0.986$, and continues to execute at $N=16, 18$ where the
full-density-matrix reference exceeds host memory. Final ranks stay at or below
$0.058\%$ of the Hilbert dimension at $N=18$, supporting the low-rank assumption
of~\cite{chen2021lret}. All other subsystems (MPI, GPU, QEC, autodiff) are verified
at the smoke-test level only; performance characterisation of these paths is
deferred to future work, as are broader circuit families, higher noise rates, and
multi-node scaling studies.

We believe this scoping is itself the contribution: a faithful, well-attributed,
reproducible implementation of a published algorithm, with one focused empirical
result and a clear roadmap for the remaining measurement work.
